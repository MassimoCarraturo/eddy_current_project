\begin{abstract}
Eddy-current testing (ECT) is an attractive in-situ probe for laser powder bed
fusion (LPBF): it is non-contact, fast, and sensitive to the electrical
conductivity. A calibrated scalar law
relates the relative conductivity change to uniaxial strain, but the residual
strain in an as-built part is a second-order tensor while a coil reports a
single scalar impedance, so reconstructing six strain components from one
reading is fundamentally under-determined. We resolve this tension with four
ingredients: a tensorial elastoresistivity model that generalises the scalar
coefficient to a fourth-order coupling tensor; a finite-element reciprocity
analysis that quantifies which conductivity components a probe senses; optimal
design of directional-probe sets --- a conductivity rosette; and a regularised
maximum-a-posteriori inversion that fuses the measurements with offline data, coming, e.g, from
thermomechanical process simulation. 
On synthetic LPBF builds a normal coil
resolves one strain degree of freedom and an in-plane rosette three; the offline
prior supplies the components the probes cannot observe.
A bonded-probe tensile campaign on additively manufactured 316L corroborates the model, resolving a strain-borne conductivity change of the predicted sign and magnitude. 
The complete workflow is implemented in an open,
tested pipeline that outputs full strain-tensor fields.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ======================================================================
Residual stress and strain are central concerns in metal additive
manufacturing: they drive distortion, cracking, and premature failure, and they
are a defining challenge of laser powder bed fusion
(LPBF)~\cite{bartlett2019}. In-situ monitoring able to map the evolving strain
field during a build would enable closed-loop process control, yet residual
strain is still measured only post-mortem, by destructive or diffraction
methods~\cite{withers2001}. Eddy-current testing (ECT) is a promising in-situ
modality~\cite{garcia2011}: a coil driven at angular frequency $\omega$ induces
eddy currents in the conductive part, and the resulting change in coil
impedance encodes the local electrical conductivity $\sigma$. Because $\sigma$
depends on elastic strain through the elastoresistive (piezoresistive) effect,
ECT senses strain indirectly.

A calibrated scalar law,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dsig}{\sigma_0} = \kappa\,\varepsilon,
  \label{eq:scalar}
\end{equation}
relates the relative change of the conductivity about its unstrained value
$\sigma_0$ to a uniaxial strain $\varepsilon$; our uniaxial-tension calibration
of 316L stainless steel yields the quoted coefficient with $R^2=0.992$.
\Cref{eq:scalar} is, however, a one-dimensional projection of a richer reality:
in three dimensions the strain $\eps$ is a symmetric second-order tensor with
six independent components, while a coil reports a single scalar. Recovering
six numbers from one is impossible without additional information.

That conductivity responds to elastic strain underpins an established line of
eddy-current residual-stress assessment: apparent-conductivity spectroscopy and
iterative inversion recover near-surface residual-stress depth profiles in
shot-peened superalloys~\cite{yu2004,abunabah2006,abunabah2007}. 
%The same work exposed a key confounder --- cold work and dislocation-density changes alter conductivity alongside elastic strain --- which is especially pronounced in LPBF microstructures and which we revisit in \cref{sec:results}.
In metal additive manufacturing, recoater-mounted ECT already monitors layer-wise
density and defects~\cite{spurek2022,monu2025}, with part-temperature
compensation shown to be essential~\cite{spurek2024}; strain, however, is not
yet mapped in~situ.

This paper develops the methodology to close that gap. Our contributions are a
tensorial elastoresistivity model that generalises $\kappa$ to a fourth-order
coupling tensor; an observability analysis, built on a verified and
cross-validated forward model, that quantifies which strain components a probe
set can resolve; an optimal directional-probe (``rosette'') design; and a
prior-informed tensor inversion, all realised in an open pipeline. The
deployment we target is sketched in \cref{fig:overview}.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. \Cref{sec:method} develops
the tensorial framework: the eddy-current forward model and its FEM verification
(\cref{sec:forward}), the elastoresistivity tensor that replaces the scalar law
(\cref{sec:tensor}), the observability analysis and directional-probe design
(\cref{sec:obs}), and the prior-informed tensor inversion and pipeline
(\cref{sec:inv}). \Cref{sec:results} reports the results and discusses them: forward-model cross-validation, the resolvable strain components, the
probe-design trade-offs, reconstruction accuracy on a synthetic build, the
recovery of the out-of-plane strain by mechanical equilibrium, and an experimental
corroboration on additively manufactured 316L. \Cref{sec:conclusions}
summarizes what was shown, with numbers, and states the study's limitations.

\section{A tensorial eddy-current framework for strain reconstruction}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Eddy-current forward model}\label{sec:forward}
\paragraph{Analytical model} For a cylindrical multi-turn coil of inner radius
$r_i$, outer radius $r_o$, length $\ell$, $n$ turns and lift-off $s$ above a
conductive half-space, the impedance follows the Dodd--Deeds
formulation~\cite{dodd1968,bowler2019,theodoulidis2006}
\begin{equation}
  Z = \frac{\mathrm{j}\omega\mu_0\pi n^2}{\ell^2 (r_o-r_i)^2}
  \int_0^\infty \frac{J^2(\alpha r_i,\alpha r_o)}{\alpha^5}
  \Big[\,2\ell + \tfrac{1}{\alpha}\big(2e^{-\alpha\ell}-2
  + P(\alpha)\,\Gamma(\alpha)\big)\Big]\,\mathrm{d}\alpha,
  \label{eq:dd}
\end{equation}
with $P(\alpha)=e^{-2\alpha(\ell+s)}+e^{-2\alpha s}-2e^{-\alpha(\ell+2s)}$, the
coil kernel $J(\alpha r_i,\alpha r_o)=\int_{\alpha r_i}^{\alpha r_o}
xJ_1(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$ built from the Bessel function $J_1$, and $\mu=\mu_0$ for
the non-magnetic 316L considered here. The half-space reflection coefficient is
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha-\gamma}{\alpha+\gamma},
  \qquad \gamma=\sqrt{\alpha^2+\mathrm{j}\omega\mu\sigma},
  \label{eq:refl}
\end{equation}
which tends to $-1$ as $\sigma\to\infty$, so eddy currents reduce the net
inductance and add resistance. Throughout we work with the normalised impedance
$Z_{\mathrm{norm}}=(Z_0-Z)/\operatorname{Im}(Z_0)$, where $Z_0$ is the
free-space (self) impedance.

\paragraph{Axisymmetric finite-element model} To treat geometries where the
half-space assumption fails --- finite parts, layered builds, defects --- we
solve the time-harmonic eddy-current problem numerically. For a coil whose axis
is normal to the surface the problem is axisymmetric, and the magnetic vector
potential reduces to its azimuthal component, $\bm A=A_\phi\,\hat{\bm\phi}$.
Inserting this ansatz into
$\nabla\times(\mu^{-1}\nabla\times\bm A)+\mathrm{j}\omega\sigma\bm A=\bm J_s$
and testing with functions $v$ of the same form yields the symmetric weak form
in the meridional $(\rho,z)$ plane:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{\mu}\!\int\!\Big[\nabla A_\phi\!\cdot\!\nabla v
  + \frac{A_\phi v}{\rho^2}\Big]\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}z
  + \mathrm{j}\omega\!\int\!\sigma A_\phi v\,\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}z
  = \int J_s\, v\,\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}z .
  \label{eq:weak}
\end{equation}
The Dirichlet condition $A_\phi=0$ is imposed on the symmetry axis ($\rho=0$,
where the azimuthal component must vanish) and on the truncated outer boundary. 
The computational
domain (\cref{fig:domain}) comprises four regions: the conductor bulk; a
near-surface conductor \emph{skin layer} meshed at one third of the skin depth
$\delta=\sqrt{2/(\omega\mu\sigma)}$, which resolves the exponential decay of
the eddy currents; the air gap; and the air above. The winding enters as a
uniform azimuthal current density $J_s=nI/S_{\mathrm{coil}}$ over its
rectangular cross-section $S_{\mathrm{coil}}=(r_o-r_i)\ell$, where $I$ is the
drive current. We discretise with $H^1$ Lagrange elements of polynomial order
three and factorise the complex symmetric system directly.

The coil impedance follows from the magnetic flux linkage of the winding,
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda = \frac{n}{S_{\mathrm{coil}}}\!\iint_{\mathrm{coil}}\!
  2\pi\rho\,A_\phi\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}z,
  \qquad Z=\mathrm{j}\omega\Lambda/I,
  \label{eq:flux}
\end{equation}
and $Z_0$ is computed by the \emph{same} model with $\sigma=0$, so
discretisation and truncation errors cancel consistently in the normalisation.
\Cref{tab:params} lists the reference parameters used in
\cref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Tensorial elastoresistivity}\label{sec:tensor}
Strain breaks the isotropy of the material: under load the conductivity is a
second-order tensor $\sigma_{ij}$ coupled to the strain through a fourth-order
\emph{elastoresistivity} tensor $\Kt$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dsig_{ij}}{\sigma_0} = K_{ijkl}\,\varepsilon_{kl}.
  \label{eq:tensor}
\end{equation}
For an isotropic base material, $\Kt$ takes the form
$K_{ijkl}=a\,\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}
+b(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})$ with only two independent
constants. Writing them as a longitudinal coefficient $\kappa_\parallel$ and a
transverse coefficient $\kappa_\perp$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dsig_{ii}}{\sigma_0}=\kappa_\parallel\varepsilon_{ii}
  + \kappa_\perp\!\!\sum_{k\neq i}\varepsilon_{kk}, \qquad
  \frac{\dsig_{ij}}{\sigma_0}=(\kappa_\parallel-\kappa_\perp)\varepsilon_{ij}
  \quad (i\neq j).
  \label{eq:coupling}
\end{equation}
The scalar law~\eqref{eq:scalar} is the uniaxial projection of
\eqref{eq:tensor}: in a uniaxial-stress test, the relative conductivity change
sensed along a unit direction $\hat{\bm n}$ is
$\hat{\bm n}^{\!\top}(\dsig/\sigma_0)\hat{\bm n}$. 
A single uniaxial test therefore fixes only
one projection of $\Kt$. We anchor $\kappa_\parallel$ so that the along-load
projection reproduces the measured scalar,
$\kappa_\parallel-2\nu\kappa_\perp$ at Poisson ratio $\nu$. Crucially,
the observability results below depend on these constants only through the
ratio $\kappa_\perp/\kappa_\parallel$; separating the two requires
multi-orientation loading. In the purely volumetric limit
$\kappa_\parallel=\kappa_\perp$, the tensor $\Kt$ is rank-one and conductivity
constrains only the volumetric strain $\operatorname{tr}\eps$.

\subsection{Observability and probe design}\label{sec:obs}
Driving-point reciprocity~\cite{auld1999} relates a small conductivity
perturbation $\delta\sigma$ to the resulting impedance change,
\begin{equation}
  \delta Z = -\frac{1}{I^2}\int \delta\sigma\,
  \bm E\!\cdot\!\bm E \,\mathrm{d}V ,
  \label{eq:recip}
\end{equation}
where $\bm E$ is the electric field driven by the coil current $I$. For the
axisymmetric coil the field is purely azimuthal,
$\bm E=-\mathrm{j}\omega A_\phi\,\hat{\bm\phi}$, so
$\bm E\!\cdot\!\bm E=E_\phi^2$: only the local in-plane (azimuthal) component
of a conductivity-tensor perturbation contributes. A normal coil is thus
structurally \emph{blind} to $\sigma_{zz}$, and its sensitivity is confined to
within a skin depth of the surface.

A directionally selective probe senses, to leading order, the effective
conductivity along its sensing direction,
\begin{equation}
  y(\hat{\bm n}) = \hat{\bm n}^{\!\top}\sig\,\hat{\bm n},
  \label{eq:proj}
\end{equation}
the conductivity analogue of a mechanical strain-gauge rosette.
\Cref{eq:proj} is a leading-order model: it assumes the probe drives current
cleanly along $\hat{\bm n}$. From the build surface this is well justified
in-plane but not out-of-plane, because eddy currents flow essentially parallel to the surface --- the axisymmetric result above makes this exact. 
A coil merely tilted above the surface gains only limited $\sigma_{zz}$
sensitivity, and genuine out-of-plane access would require edge or side probing,
elongated or rotating-field probes, or multi-frequency depth profiling. We
therefore propose an in-plane rosette design --- which resolves the three in-plane components --- and supply the unobserved out-of-plane components from the offline
prior of \cref{sec:inv} and from mechanical equilibrium.

Stacking $M$ probes gives a linear map
$\bm y=\mathsf{M}\,\bm\sigma_{\mathrm v}$ from the conductivity Voigt vector
$\bm\sigma_{\mathrm v}$ to the measurement vector $\bm y$; composing with $\Kt$
yields the strain map $\bm y=\mathsf{A}\,\eps_{\mathrm v}$ with
$\mathsf{A}=\sigma_0\,\mathsf{M}\,\Kt$. The singular value decomposition of
$\mathsf A$ exposes the resolvable rank, the conditioning, and the null space
--- the blind strain directions. We design probe sets by minimising the
condition number of $\mathsf A$ subject to a rank constraint; the resolvable
ceiling is $\operatorname{rank}\Kt$, so directional variation helps only as long as the elastoresistive response is anisotropic.

\subsection{Prior-informed tensor inversion and pipeline}\label{sec:inv}
Realistic probe sets are rank-deficient. We therefore estimate the strain by
maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) inversion~\cite{aster2018}, fusing the measurements
with an offline prior $\eps_{\mathrm{prior}}$ --- an estimate of the as-built
stress/strain field, for which a thermomechanical process simulation is one
example source. With Gaussian
measurement noise of standard deviation $\sigma_d$ and an independent,
identically distributed Gaussian prior of standard deviation $\sigma_p$,
\begin{equation}
  \eps^\star = \eps_{\mathrm{prior}}
  + \big(\mathsf A^{\!\top}\mathsf A + \lambda \mathsf I\big)^{-1}
  \mathsf A^{\!\top}\big(\bm y - \mathsf A\,\eps_{\mathrm{prior}}\big),
  \qquad \lambda=\sigma_d^2/\sigma_p^2 .
  \label{eq:map}
\end{equation}
The resolution matrix
$\mathsf R=(\mathsf A^{\!\top}\mathsf A+\lambda\mathsf I)^{-1}
\mathsf A^{\!\top}\mathsf A$ quantifies, component by component, the fraction
supplied by the data: a diagonal entry of $1$ marks a measurement-determined
component, $0$ one taken from the prior. Where only a normal-coil scalar is
available, the estimate degrades gracefully to the volumetric strain through
the invariant coupling $\kappa_v=(\kappa_\parallel+2\kappa_\perp)/3$. A full
prior covariance estimated from an ensemble of such offline models would
replace the scalar $\lambda$ and is a natural refinement.

\subsection{Experimental setup and conductivity calibration}\label{sec:experiment}
The framework's central premise --- that a surface eddy-current probe resolves a
strain-borne conductivity change --- was tested on additively manufactured 316L.
Twenty-three flat dogbone coupons were produced by laser powder bed fusion at the
University of Pavia; eleven (\cref{fig:setup}) were loaded to failure in uniaxial
tension on a servo-hydraulic frame. Each coupon is \SI{116}{\milli\metre} long
with a \SI{10}{\milli\metre}$\times$\SI{2}{\milli\metre} gauge (area
${\approx}\SI{20.5}{\milli\metre\squared}$, from the part CAD), so the load cell
fixes the gauge stress directly. An eddy-current probe was bonded to the gauge
surface and driven at \SI{240.4}{\kilo\hertz} throughout each test.

The bonded mount fixes the nominal lift-off, but residual contact variation still
biases the raw signal, so we calibrate it out. A separate frequency--lift-off
sweep (\SIrange{231.5}{297.6}{\kilo\hertz}; lift-offs
\SIrange{0}{1000}{\micro\metre}) records the response over that grid, and a
radial-basis interpolant inverts it to map a measured
$(\operatorname{Re},\operatorname{Im})$ pair to an effective frequency
$f_{\mathrm{eff}}$. Because the eddy-current response depends on the product
$\omega\sigma$ --- the skin depth scales as $(\omega\sigma)^{-1/2}$ --- at the
fixed drive frequency $f_0$ a conductivity change is indistinguishable from a
frequency change, so $f_{\mathrm{eff}}/f_0=\sigma/\sigma_0$ and
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Delta\sigma}{\sigma_0}=\frac{\Delta f_{\mathrm{eff}}}{f_{\mathrm{eff}}} .
  \label{eq:feff}
\end{equation}
The effective frequency is thus a lift-off-invariant conductivity proxy; samples
whose response leaves the calibrated grid are discarded.

\section{Results and discussion}\label{sec:results}
% ======================================================================
\paragraph{Forward-model verification} The finite-element model is mesh converged: order-three and order-four solutions agree to five significant figures. 
%It is also internally consistent: the resistance extracted from the
%flux linkage~\eqref{eq:flux} equals an independent volume integral of the
%ohmic loss, $\tfrac12\!\int\sigma|\bm E|^2\,\mathrm{d}V$, to better than
%$10^{-5}$. 
For the reference parameters of \cref{tab:params}, the air
impedance is purely inductive, $Z_0=\mathrm{j}\,5.18\times10^{4}\,\si{\ohm}$,
while the loaded impedance, $Z=(622+\mathrm{j}\,3.73\times10^{4})\,\si{\ohm}$,
shows that eddy currents add resistance and reduce reactance. Across five conductivities spanning the locus, analytical and
finite-element normalised impedances agree to within \SI{0.9}{\percent} in
resistance and \SIrange{2.8}{5.8}{\percent} in reactance (\cref{fig:impedance});
the reactance residual grows toward low conductivity, where the larger skin
depth feels the finite computational domain versus the analytical half-space. The integral~\eqref{eq:dd} is converged for
$k_{\max}\sim6000$, within a stable plateau before the oscillatory kernel
degrades the quadrature, and the reciprocity relation~\eqref{eq:recip} matches
finite-difference re-solves with a ratio that approaches unity as the
perturbation shrinks (\cref{fig:femconv}).

\paragraph{What a probe senses} The sensitivity density $|E_\phi|^2$ inside
the specimen decays from the surface over a skin depth
(\cref{fig:kernel}), and the excitation frequency tunes that penetration:
\SI{120}{\kilo\hertz} reaches markedly deeper than \SI{480}{\kilo\hertz}
(\cref{fig:depth}). Because the induced field is in-plane, a single coil
senses only the in-plane mean conductivity, and the effective $\kappa$ it
reports depends on the sensing direction --- swinging from $-0.35$ along the
load to $+0.06$ transverse to it (\cref{fig:rosette}). Stacking directional
probes and analysing the strain map $\mathsf A$ quantifies the resolving
power: a normal coil resolves one strain degree of freedom and an in-plane
rosette three (\cref{tab:obs}); from the build surface the out-of-plane
components remain unobservable. Optimising the in-plane angles improves the
conditioning from $2.57$ ($0/45/90$) and $2.18$ ($0/60/120$) to $1.93$ at the
angles $\theta=\{22^\circ,90^\circ,158^\circ\}$ (\cref{fig:rosette_design}).

\paragraph{Material limit on observability} The ceiling
$\operatorname{rank}\Kt$ is set by the material itself. Sweeping the
elastoresistivity ratio $\kappa_\perp/\kappa_\parallel$ shows that the
resolvable degrees of freedom stay full for any anisotropic
response, while the conditioning of $\mathsf A$ degrades smoothly toward the
volumetric limit $\kappa_\perp\to\kappa_\parallel$, where the rank collapses
to one. At the calibration-anchored ratio of ${\approx}0.21$ the in-plane
rosette is well conditioned, so the probe design is insensitive to the
experimentally still-open split between $\kappa_\parallel$ and $\kappa_\perp$.

\paragraph{Tensor reconstruction} On a synthetic build-height profile with a
deliberately biased prior, the inversion~\eqref{eq:map} corrects the in-plane
components from the rosette data, while the out-of-plane components follow the
prior (\cref{fig:inversion}), exactly as the resolution-matrix diagonal
predicts. The in-plane rosette reduces the strain RMSE from
$9.4\times10^{-4}$ (prior only) to $3.9\times10^{-4}$; the out-of-plane
$\varepsilon_{zz}$ is then recovered by the equilibrium closure below. Embedded in the pipeline, the method
reconstructs full tensor fields; \cref{fig:fieldeqv,fig:fieldvol} show the
von-Mises-equivalent and volumetric strain fields recovered on a
$50\times36$ grid of a synthetic build, the volumetric strain evolving from
compressive near the base plate to tensile higher up. Each grid point is
currently inverted independently; folding the finite footprint of the
sensitivity kernel (\cref{fig:kernel}) into $\mathsf A$ is the consistent next
step for a quantitative treatment.

\paragraph{Lifting the out-of-plane blindness by equilibrium} The skin effect
denies the surface probes any direct grip on the out-of-plane components, yet
mechanics supplies them. On a traction-free surface with normal $z$, equilibrium
forces a plane-stress state ($\sigma_{zz}=\sigma_{xz}=\sigma_{yz}=0$), and
isotropic Hooke's law fixes the out-of-plane strain exactly from the in-plane
components,
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon_{zz}=-\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}\,(\varepsilon_{xx}+\varepsilon_{yy}),
  \qquad \varepsilon_{xz}=\varepsilon_{yz}=0 ,
  \label{eq:closure}
\end{equation}
with Poisson ratio $\nu$. For a build whose residual field varies mainly through
the height (laterally homogeneous), interior equilibrium propagates this
plane-stress state to every depth, so \cref{eq:closure} holds through the column;
lateral gradients make $\sigma_{zz}$ nonzero and require the full elastic
boundary-value problem, the natural role of an offline model such as a
thermomechanical simulation, which we discharge next.
\Cref{eq:closure} is three linear constraints on $\eps_{\mathrm v}$; stacking them
with the measurement map renders the rank-deficient inverse problem full-rank, so
an in-plane rosette resolves all six components --- three from data, three from
equilibrium. On the synthetic build of \cref{fig:equilibrium} the
$\varepsilon_{zz}$ resolution rises from $0.05$ (data only) to $1.00$, and its
reconstruction error falls from $1.0\times10^{-3}$ --- locked on a biased prior
--- to $1.1\times10^{-4}$, the measurement-noise floor. The constraint applies to
the \emph{elastic} strain, which in practice must be separated from cold-work and
thermal contributions to the conductivity.

\paragraph{From a surface identity to field equilibrium} \Cref{eq:closure} is a
pointwise relation on the free surface; in the interior it holds only where the
residual state is laterally uniform. Over the clamped baseplate that anchors every
LPBF part, and near overhangs and melt-pool boundaries, $\sigma_{zz}$ no longer
vanishes below the surface and the closure drifts. We make this quantitative by
solving the balance law $\nabla\!\cdot\!\sig=\bm0$ on a vertical $(x,z)$ slice with
the \texttt{mlhp} hp-finite-element
kernel.\footnote{\url{https://gitlab.com/hpfem/code/mlhp}} A laterally varying
in-plane eigenstrain that vanishes on the free faces drives the residual
state---reducing the Mura problem to a single equivalent body force
$\bm b=-\nabla\!\cdot\!\sig^{\ast}$ with the base clamped---so the solve stands in
transparently for the thermomechanical twin. At the traction-free top the
finite-element field reproduces \cref{eq:closure} to within \SI{0.2}{\percent},
but \cref{fig:equilibrium_field} shows the surface closure parting from the
equilibrated profile with depth: the error reaches ${\approx}\,\SI{87}{\percent}$
of $\varepsilon_{zz}$ at mid-height and inverts in sign over the lower quarter,
where $|\sigma_{zz}|$ has climbed by nearly four orders of magnitude from its
surface value. The field solve recovers $\varepsilon_{zz}$ throughout. The
operational reading sharpens accordingly: a surface rosette fixes the in-plane
strain, and an equilibrium engine---the algebraic closure at the surface, the full
boundary-value problem beneath it---supplies the out-of-plane component.

\paragraph{Feasibility on LPBF 316L} Three physical realities set the
instrument requirements. \emph{Signal:} residual elastic strains of
$1$--$2\times10^{-3}$ produce a relative conductivity change of only
$3$--$7\times10^{-4}$ through \cref{eq:scalar}.
\emph{Temperature, the dominant confounder:} 316L has resistivity
${\approx}\SI{0.74}{\micro\ohm\metre}$
($\sigma\approx\SI{1.35}{\mega\siemens\per\metre}$) with a temperature
coefficient of order $10^{-3}\,\si{\per\kelvin}$~\cite{nist316l}, so a drift
of ${\sim}\SI{1}{\kelvin}$ shifts $\sigma$ by as much as the entire strain
signal --- consistent with in-situ ECT studies that found part temperature
dominant and compensation essential~\cite{spurek2024}. Differential or
reference-channel probes, multi-frequency lift-off and temperature
separation, and tight thermal referencing are therefore prerequisites; cold
work and dislocation density add a microstructure-borne confounder that
co-varies with thermal history. \emph{Depth:} at the realistic 316L
conductivity the skin depth is ${\approx}\SI{0.9}{\milli\metre}$ at
\SI{240}{\kilo\hertz} and ${\approx}\SI{0.4}{\milli\metre}$ at
\SI{1}{\mega\hertz} --- far larger than a $30$--$\SI{50}{\micro\metre}$
layer, so each reading averages $20$--$30$ layers, and layer-resolved
tomography would require multi-frequency depth inversion. The reference-coil
study above (higher, generic conductivity) is correspondingly a verification
case, not a 316L prediction. None of this undermines the framework; it
specifies the instrument --- a thermally referenced, multi-frequency,
directional-probe rosette.

\paragraph{Experimental corroboration} Applying the lift-off-compensated proxy
\eqref{eq:feff} of \cref{sec:experiment} to the bonded-probe records yields the
relative conductivity change of \cref{fig:experiment}. Across the specimens the
conductivity \emph{decreases} monotonically under tension --- the sign demanded
by $\kappa<0$ --- by a median of ${\approx}\SI{0.7}{\percent}$ at the peak load
(${\approx}\SI{490}{\mega\pascal}$, i.e.\ at yield), consistent in magnitude with
the scalar law \eqref{eq:scalar} at the percent-level strains reached. The change
is irreversible --- it does not recover over the long plastic plateau --- so a
tensile-to-failure test is dominated by the plastic and cold-work response
flagged above, not by the reversible elastoresistive coupling. Two artefacts of
this first campaign compound the point: the elastic ramp lasts under
\SI{10}{\second} of a ${\sim}\SI{100}{\second}$ test, and the eddy-current and
tensile acquisitions were started a few seconds apart, so a clean elastic slope
--- and hence a direct measurement of $\kappa$ --- cannot be read from these
records. Isolating the elastic coefficient therefore calls for slow, controlled
elastic-range loading with synchronized, thermally referenced acquisition, as the
instrument requirements above already imply. The campaign nonetheless confirms
the central premise: bonded eddy-current sensing resolves a strain-borne
conductivity change of the expected sign and order of magnitude in real LPBF 316L.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
% ======================================================================
A scalar impedance constrains one projection of the conductivity tensor;
reconstructing the strain tensor therefore requires measurement diversity and,
for whatever remains unobservable from the surface, prior information fused by
regularised inversion. Within this framework: a verified axisymmetric
finite-element model agrees with the analytical Dodd--Deeds solution to within
\SI{0.9}{\percent} in resistance and \SIrange{2.8}{5.8}{\percent} in reactance
across the conductivity range; a normal
coil resolves one strain degree of freedom and an in-plane rosette three (best
conditioning $1.93$); and the maximum-a-posteriori inversion reduces the strain
RMSE on a synthetic build from $9.4\times10^{-4}$ (prior only) to
$3.9\times10^{-4}$ with the in-plane rosette, the out-of-plane components
supplied by the prior and mechanical equilibrium.

Two limitations bound the present study. Out-of-plane observability is weak from
the build surface; mechanical equilibrium closes much of the gap --- the
free-surface condition \eqref{eq:closure} recovers $\varepsilon_{zz}$ from the
in-plane rosette, and the bulk field with lateral gradients follows from the full
elastic solve (\cref{fig:equilibrium_field}) --- but this transfers the burden to
a clean separation of elastic
strain from cold-work and thermal effects. And the elastoresistivity constants
are calibrated
only up to one uniaxial projection; multi-orientation tests are needed to
separate $\kappa_\parallel$ from $\kappa_\perp$ and to characterise the
as-built anisotropy of $\sigma_0$ in columnar LPBF microstructures. Future
work targets a three-dimensional anisotropic eddy-current model to quantify
out-of-plane sensitivity directly, experimental validation against
multi-orientation and benchmark~\cite{burke1988} data, and deployment of the
pipeline for in-situ monitoring.
